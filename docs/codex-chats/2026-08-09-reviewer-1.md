Yes—there’s something publishable here, but not yet a defensible biological forecasting result.

The strongest scientific story may actually be:

> Simple, constrained reaction–diffusion models perform about as well as more complex physics-informed hybrids when assimilating opportunistic spotted-lanternfly reports.

That is interesting because:

- You have 60,142 occurrence records and 20 explicit model ablations.
- Climate-weighted growth and land/water resistance rank first.
- The learned residual ranks fourth, while the full hybrid ranks fifteenth.
- Scores are extremely close: 89.4–90.2 overall and only 90.0–90.2 among the top five.

That last point means you cannot currently claim D05 is “best.” You may be seeing statistical equivalence, threshold-tuning noise, or insufficiently discriminating validation. But a properly tested finding that added complexity provides little benefit would itself be valuable.

What prevents a full research paper today:

- Each model’s threshold is optimized on the same 2021–2025 window used to report performance.
- Monthly backcasts assimilate the reports they display, so visual agreement is not independent validation.
- Unreported cells are pseudo-absences, and urban proximity is a weak reporting-effort proxy.
- There is no spatially blocked, leave-state-out, or genuinely prospective evaluation.
- There are no bootstrap confidence intervals showing whether the 0.1–0.3 score differences mean anything.
- GBIF records represent reporting activity—not unbiased abundance, occupancy, or absence.

The research paper I would build is:

**“Evidence-assimilating reaction–diffusion ensembles for invasion-front mapping from opportunistic occurrence records.”**

The central question would be whether constrained diffusion models produce calibrated, interpretable short-horizon spread estimates under observation bias—and whether neural complexity improves them.

To make that publishable:

1. Lock 2025 or 2026 as a completely untouched test set.
2. Tune thresholds only inside earlier nested validation years.
3. Add spatial block and leave-state-out tests.
4. Compare against simple persistence, distance-buffer, Fisher–KPP, and standard species-distribution baselines.
5. Replace the urban proxy with a real sampling-effort surface where possible.
6. Bootstrap by year, region, and record source to obtain confidence intervals.
7. Report calibration, PR-AUC, recall at fixed survey effort, and spatial error—not only the custom composite score.
8. Keep backcasts, forecasts, interpolation, and observations rigorously separated in figures.

There is also a nearer-term software paper. The app fits the scope of [SoftwareX](https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/softwarex), which publishes open research software with a short descriptive paper and reusable open-source distribution. ## Major strengths

- The first-report-cell endpoint correctly removes the overwhelming persistence credit in cumulative occurrence maps.
- The paper consistently avoids claims about occupancy, abundance, calibration, or causal transport effects.
- Numerical values in the results narrative and tables agree with the generated CSV/JSON artifacts.

- The manuscript clearly recognizes that no model dominates: OG-RDE leads mean annual AP, transport RD leads block AP, and the full model leads partial 2026.

## Major concerns

1. **This is not a genuine historical forecast using information available at each origin.** The observations were downloaded in August 2026 and partitioned by `eventDate`. A record collected in 2020 but uploaded after 2021 is treated as if it were available at the 2021 origin. The host surface is likewise fetched from a current GBIF snapshot and used in every historical fold. The paper’s “only information available through \(t-1\)” claim is therefore unsupported.

2. **The spatial analysis is not spatial cross-validation or geographic generalization.** OG-RDE trains on earlier examples from all spatial blocks, including the same blocks later evaluated. Computing AP within blocks is useful stratification, but it does not measure transfer to unseen geography.

3. **The bootstrap does not preserve the dependence structure.** The code resamples 64 block-year rows independently even though only 20 spatial blocks recur over two to four years. Calling this a cluster bootstrap is inaccurate and likely makes intervals too narrow. Blocks, years, or a defensible two-way structure should be resampled as clusters.

4. **The legacy-versus-frontier performance drop is not an apples-to-apples comparison.** The legacy value near 0.90 is a tuned composite of recall, F1, precision, and Brier on cumulative maps; the new value near 0.50 is AP on first reports. The manuscript attributes the entire difference to endpoint correction without recomputing the same metric and model under both endpoints.

5. **The numerical physics is insufficiently specified and contains a boundary artifact.** `np.roll` imposes periodic boundaries, connecting opposite edges of the study domain. The implemented flow, `laplacian(u * resistance)` multiplied by a spatial rate, is also not the displayed \(\nabla\cdot(D\nabla u)\) operator. There is no parameter table, units, stability analysis, convergence check, or boundary-condition discussion.

6. **Model selection is not convincingly firewalled.** Values such as \(C=0.08\), Gaussian scales, positive weighting, physics constants, and the 50/50 transport fusion appear fixed in the final script but have no nested tuning procedure or timestamped preregistration. Partial 2026 therefore cannot be called an independent holdout without a frozen pre-2026 specification.

7. **The benchmark is too narrow for a state-of-the-art claim.** It compares in-house heuristic variants and a distance kernel but not a strong non-physics spatiotemporal hazard, prior-year KDE, reporting-only model, or an implementable published spread approach such as PoPS. OG-RDE does not significantly beat the leading mechanistic models.

8. **The stated record count is inaccurate.** The bundle contains 62,791 records, but only 60,083 fall inside the stated domain and 2014–2026 range. The manifest records the bundle length rather than the number actually analyzed.

1. **The app’s headline model ranking is not the frozen benchmark emphasized by the paper.**  
   The UI prominently presents “TOP 5 · TEMPORAL BACKTEST,” ranks D05/D11/etc. by recall, F1 and a legacy composite ([index.html]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/index.html:60), [app.js]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/app.js:636)). The paper explicitly says assimilating playback is not a benchmark source ([lanterntrace.tex]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/research/paper/lanterntrace.tex:143)). The existing caveat is helpful but insufficient: staff could reasonably conclude D05 is the scientifically validated winner. Label this as a legacy/display-model ranking and surface the frozen OG-RDE, covariate-hazard, Transport-RD and Cook-2021 evaluation in an Evaluation tab or linked panel.

2. **Frozen validation is effectively absent from the app.**  
   The paper’s most decision-relevant evidence—2024/2025 AP, top-5% survey yield, block intervals and frozen maps—is available only in the PDF/artifacts. Meanwhile, the Evidence panel shows unexplained “Image classifier 0.8476” and “Snapshot sensitivity 0.5345” values ([index.html]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/index.html:43)). These should be replaced with or subordinated to the documented surveillance benchmark, with metric definitions and provenance.

3. **The monthly timeline hides the exact current timestep.**  
   The app updates `2025 Dec`/`evidence step` internally ([app.js]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/app.js:599)), but CSS hides the complete date/phase display ([styles.css]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/styles.css:315)). Users can see the geometry change but cannot reliably state which month they are viewing.

4. **A public clone is not staff-runnable without substantial technical setup.**  
   The committed repo omits the approximately 14 MB observations and 3 MB model display artifact. The documented workflow requires Node, Electron, Python and scientific Python dependencies, but the README provides no Python-environment or dependency-install instructions ([README.md]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/README.md:11), [package.json]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/package.json:7)). There is no downloadable application release; the README confirms the build is neither notarized nor distributed. Provide a packaged research release/sample artifact, or a tested one-command bootstrap with explicit platform and dependency requirements.

5. **The README advertises controls that are not present.**  
   It claims built-in place search and JSON export ([README.md]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/README.md:30)). Functions exist in [app.js]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/app.js:912), but current HTML has neither control and interaction setup never wires them. Restore the controls or remove the claims and dead paths. The README screenshot is also stale: it depicts the older prospective-envelope/default presentation rather than the current forecast-off/model-lab UI.

6. **Prototype decision layers need stronger visible provenance.**  
   “Surveillance sites” is a hard-coded seven-point sentinel list ([data.js]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/data.js:74)), and action cards are fixed recommendations. A footer caveat exists, but the layer itself reads like a real sentinel network. Relabel these everywhere as illustrative/proposed and expose their source or construction.

7. **Accessibility is below professional readiness.**  
   Most layer toggle buttons lack accessible names and state attributes ([index.html]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/index.html:33)); the accessibility tree exposed several simply as “button.” Model and settings copy drops to 6–9 px ([styles.css]($HOME/Downloads/lanterntrace-explorer/styles.css:511)), which is too small for sustained operational use. Add accessible names/`aria-pressed`, keyboard focus treatment and readable minimum text sizing.

8. **Public reuse terms are undefined.**  
   The [public repository](https://github.com/axel-slid/lanterntrace-explorer) has no committed license. That blocks agency adoption and redistribution even if the software is technically usable.

### Nice-to-have improvements

- Cluster or progressively aggregate occurrence points; dense invaded areas become a nearly solid white cloud at useful zoom levels.
- Add a two-minute “program manager” tour explaining what the tool can prioritize and what it cannot infer.
- Support saved/shareable views and operational exports once the promised export is restored.
- Cache or package a fallback basemap for degraded/offline field connectivity.
- Add CI-built, signed releases and complete the archive DOI/GBIF derived-dataset DOI work already identified in the paper.

My strict recommendation: **strong research demonstration, suitable for expert-guided demos and paper review; not yet ready for unattended surveillance-program deployment.** The fastest route toward 9 is to unify the app’s evaluation story with the frozen benchmark, restore visible temporal context, and ship a self-contained public build.
